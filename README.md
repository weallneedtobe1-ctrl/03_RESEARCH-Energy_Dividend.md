1. Introduction

Every economic activity requires resources.

Among those resources, energy is fundamental.

Production, transportation, communication, computation, manufacturing, administration, and human activity all require energy directly or indirectly.

Conventional economic measurement often records energy as an input, cost, or environmental variable.

HGDP proposes an additional question:

How much useful human value can an economic system create while using less unnecessary energy?

This question forms the basis of the:

Energy Dividend

⸻

2. Definition

The Energy Dividend (ED) is defined as:

The measurable reduction in net energy required to achieve an equivalent or improved useful outcome within a defined system boundary.

The critical elements are:

Net

Energy

Required

Useful Outcome

Defined System Boundary

Without these elements, an energy-saving claim may be incomplete.

⸻

3. Core Equation

The initial conceptual equation is:

ED = Baseline System Energy − New System Energy

Where:

* ED = Energy Dividend;
* Baseline System Energy = energy required under the reference system;
* New System Energy = energy required under the new system.

A positive result indicates a reduction in energy requirement.

A negative result indicates increased net energy consumption.

⸻

4. Device Energy vs System Energy

HGDP establishes a critical distinction:

Device Energy ≠ Social Energy

A smartphone, computer, or AI system consumes electricity.

However, that device may reduce:

* travel;
* paperwork;
* transportation;
* physical work;
* repeated communication;
* institutional processing.

Therefore measuring only electricity consumed by the device may produce an incomplete assessment.

The relevant question is:

What happened to total system energy after the intervention?

⸻

5. Direct Energy

Direct energy refers to energy consumed directly by the measured activity.

Examples include:

* electricity;
* fuel;
* thermal energy;
* mechanical energy.

Direct energy should be measured using appropriate instrumentation wherever possible.

⸻

6. Indirect Energy

Economic activities may also require indirect energy.

Examples include:

* manufacturing equipment;
* transportation infrastructure;
* data centers;
* network infrastructure;
* buildings;
* maintenance;
* supply chains.

The relevance of indirect energy depends on the defined system boundary.

Researchers must state whether indirect energy is included or excluded.

⸻

7. System Boundary

Energy measurement is meaningful only when the system boundary is defined.

Possible boundaries include:

Device Boundary

Measures only the device.

Task Boundary

Measures the energy required to complete a particular task.

Organizational Boundary

Measures the energy consumed by an organization.

Sector Boundary

Measures a sector or industry.

National Boundary

Measures energy consumption across an economy.

Life-Cycle Boundary

Includes relevant energy throughout production, use, and disposal.

Different boundaries may produce different results.

Neither should be presented as universally correct without qualification.

⸻

8. Baseline Requirement

Every Energy Dividend measurement requires a baseline.

Examples:

* previous technology;
* conventional process;
* historical average;
* control group;
* industry benchmark;
* counterfactual model.

The baseline must be documented.

Without a baseline:

Energy Consumption

can be measured,

but:

Energy Dividend

cannot be established.

⸻

9. Equivalent Outcome Requirement

Energy savings should not be recognized merely because less energy was consumed.

The useful outcome must be:

* equivalent;
* improved;
* or otherwise appropriately adjusted.

For example:

A system that consumes 50% less energy but produces 70% less useful output cannot automatically be classified as more energy productive.

⸻

10. Energy Productivity

HGDP proposes:

Energy Productivity = Useful Output / Energy Required

This may be compared with:

Liberation Energy Productivity = Verified Human Value / Energy Required

The second metric extends the question beyond output.

⸻

11. Net Energy Saving

The preferred concept is:

Net Energy Saving

rather than:

Local Energy Saving

For example:

A digital service may eliminate physical paperwork but increase:

* data-center energy;
* network energy;
* device usage.

The final measurement should consider the relevant system boundary.

⸻

12. AI Energy

AI introduces a particularly important measurement problem.

An AI response may appear to require little human energy.

But the full system may consume energy through:

* model computation;
* data centers;
* cooling;
* storage;
* networking;
* user devices;
* human verification.

Therefore:

AI Response Speed ≠ AI Energy Efficiency

and:

AI Efficiency ≠ Automatically Net Energy Saving

⸻

13. AI Energy Measurement

An AI-related Energy Dividend experiment should ideally measure:

Baseline Human Process

versus:

AI-Assisted Process

including:

* computation;
* device energy;
* network energy where measurable;
* human verification;
* correction;
* repeated processing.

The final result should be reported with its system boundary.

⸻

14. Human Energy

Human physical and cognitive effort is difficult to convert directly into electricity-equivalent units.

HGDP therefore distinguishes:

Physical Energy

from:

Social Energy

Social Energy may include human effort, coordination, information processing, and institutional activity.

These should not automatically be converted into kWh.

A future methodology may develop additional normalization methods.

⸻

15. Social Energy

Conceptually:

Social Energy = Human Effort + Institutional Effort + Physical Energy + Information Effort

This is an analytical representation.

It is not an established physical unit.

Each component requires separate measurement.

⸻

16. Energy and Human Time

Energy and time are connected but not identical.

A system may:

* save energy but increase human time;
* save time but increase energy;
* save both;
* increase both.

HGDP therefore measures them separately.

The objective is not to force every resource into a single unit.

⸻

17. Four Basic Outcomes

An intervention can produce four major combinations.

Type A

Time ↓ + Energy ↓

Potentially strong Human Liberation outcome.

Type B

Time ↓ + Energy ↑

Potentially useful if the value of time released justifies the additional energy, but this requires measurement.

Type C

Time ↑ + Energy ↓

Energy benefit with possible human burden.

Type D

Time ↑ + Energy ↑

Potential negative outcome.

The final assessment must consider the broader human-value system.

⸻

18. Energy Rebound

Efficiency can increase total consumption.

For example:

If computation becomes cheaper, people may use much more computation.

The energy required per task may decrease while total energy consumption increases.

Therefore HGDP requires two measurements:

Energy per Unit

and:

Total System Energy

Both are necessary to evaluate rebound effects.

⸻

19. Jevons-Type Effect

HGDP recognizes the possibility that greater efficiency can increase total demand.

Therefore:

Efficiency Gain ≠ Automatically Total Energy Reduction

This is especially relevant to:

* computing;
* transportation;
* manufacturing;
* communication;
* AI.

The effect must be measured rather than assumed.

⸻

20. Energy Dividend and Economic Growth

Economic growth may occur while energy consumption:

* increases;
* decreases;
* remains stable.

HGDP therefore investigates:

Decoupling

between useful economic or human value and energy consumption.

A key research question is:

Can verified human value increase faster than the energy required to produce it?

⸻

21. Human Liberation Energy Ratio

A conceptual metric is:

HLER = Verified Human Value / System Energy

Where:

* HLER = Human Liberation Energy Ratio.

The metric attempts to describe how much verified human value is generated per unit of system energy.

It remains experimental.

⸻

22. Energy and Resource Restoration

Energy savings may also reduce resource pressure.

For example:

Lower energy demand may reduce:

* fuel extraction;
* infrastructure demand;
* emissions;
* material consumption.

These secondary effects should not be counted automatically.

They require separate evidence.

⸻

23. Energy and Transportation

Transportation provides a useful experimental case.

A digital process may replace a physical journey.

The measurement should compare:

Physical Process

with:

Digital Process

including relevant:

* travel energy;
* infrastructure energy;
* device energy;
* network energy;
* human time.

The purpose is to determine the net system effect.

⸻

24. Energy and Public Services

Government services may provide another major application.

Examples:

* digital identity;
* online licensing;
* electronic documents;
* digital tax services;
* remote public consultations.

Potential benefits include:

* reduced travel;
* reduced waiting;
* reduced paperwork;
* reduced administrative processing.

These should be measured rather than assumed.

⸻

25. Energy and Information

Information systems can reduce physical resource consumption.

For example:

Physical Document

may become:

Digital Document

But digitization does not mean zero energy consumption.

The appropriate comparison is:

Physical System Energy

versus:

Digital System Energy

within a defined boundary.

⸻

26. Energy and Knowledge

Knowledge can sometimes substitute for physical resource consumption.

For example:

A researcher may avoid unnecessary experimentation after obtaining reliable prior knowledge.

However, the knowledge itself required:

* computation;
* storage;
* communication;
* human work.

Therefore the net energy effect must be measured.

⸻

27. Energy Dividend Distribution

Energy savings create an economic question:

Who receives the benefit?

Possible beneficiaries include:

* consumers;
* workers;
* businesses;
* governments;
* infrastructure owners;
* society.

Lower system energy costs may become:

* lower prices;
* higher profits;
* higher wages;
* public savings;
* additional services.

HGDP therefore treats energy efficiency and value distribution as related but separate questions.

⸻

28. Energy Ownership

Energy infrastructure can be highly concentrated.

HGDP therefore proposes examining:

* ownership;
* access;
* pricing;
* infrastructure control;
* technological dependence.

An energy-efficient system may still create high concentration of economic power.

Therefore:

Energy Efficiency ≠ Automatically Economic Liberation

⸻

29. Measurement Protocol

A basic Energy Dividend experiment should record:

1. Baseline process
2. Baseline energy
3. New process
4. New energy
5. Useful output
6. Quality
7. Human time
8. System boundary
9. Measurement instrument
10. Uncertainty
11. Rebound effects
12. Verification status

⸻

30. Measurement Hierarchy

Energy evidence should distinguish:

Estimated Energy

Calculated from assumptions.

Measured Energy

Directly measured.

Audited Energy

Independently examined.

Verified Energy

Independently verified.

The terms must not be used interchangeably.

⸻

31. No False Precision

HGDP prohibits false precision.

If energy consumption cannot be measured accurately, the result should be reported as:

* estimate;
* range;
* uncertainty interval;
* qualitative finding.

A highly precise number based on weak assumptions is less useful than a transparent range.

⸻

32. Negative Results

HGDP requires negative results to remain visible.

If a technology:

* consumes more energy;
* increases total system demand;
* creates significant rebound;
* fails to preserve output;

the result must be recorded.

The purpose of the framework is measurement, not confirmation of the theory.

⸻

33. Experimental Hypothesis

The initial Energy Dividend hypothesis is:

Technological and organizational improvements can increase verified human value while reducing the energy required to achieve useful outcomes.

This is a hypothesis.

It must be tested empirically.

⸻

34. Research Questions

HGDP proposes the following research questions:

1. How much energy can digital systems actually save?
2. How much additional energy do digital systems create?
3. What is the net effect?
4. How large are rebound effects?
5. How much human time is returned?
6. Does energy efficiency translate into public value?
7. Who receives the resulting economic benefit?
8. Does efficiency reduce or increase power concentration?

⸻

35. Energy Dividend and the Human Liberation Economy

The Human Liberation Economy does not seek:

Minimum Energy at Any Cost.

It seeks:

Maximum Verified Human Value per Necessary Energy.

This distinction is essential.

An economy that consumes little energy but provides little human capability is not necessarily more successful.

An economy that creates enormous output while consuming enormous unnecessary energy may also be inefficient.

The objective is the balance.

⸻

36. Final Principle

Energy is not valuable merely because less of it is consumed.

Energy becomes economically meaningful when its use creates useful human outcomes.

The central question is therefore:

How much verified human value can humanity create from each unit of necessary energy?

And beyond that:

How much unnecessary energy can an intelligent economy eliminate without reducing human capability, quality, safety, or dignity?
